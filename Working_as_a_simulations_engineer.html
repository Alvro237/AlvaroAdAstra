Working as a Simulations Engineer

Eleven months across three simulation disciplines at Madrid Space Europe — structural, thermal, and CFD. Here's what it actually looks like.

CFD FEM Thermal OpenFOAM ESATAN HyperMesh OptiStruct
Overview

What to expect as a simulations engineer?

During my time at Madrid Space, I rotated through the simulation departments: structural, thermal, and CFD. Since everything happened in just 11 months, I am still a beginner with much to learn. However, that time was enough to get a global picture of what working in simulations is really like, and I want to share it for anyone considering this path.

Performing simulations of real objects was very appealing to me even before I started. The ability to iterate a design, optimise it, and predict its behaviour without manufacturing a prototype is enormously valuable. I also used this experience to deepen my understanding of the physics — most of it was covered in the aerospace course, but simulations made the theory feel real. Seeing those colourful results was genuinely fulfilling.

Switching between projects taught me to adapt quickly, manage evolving requirements, and stay efficient in fast-paced environments. Broadening across disciplines rather than specialising too early turned out to be a real advantage early in a career.

01 · Structural

Structural Engineering

This was the first branch I dealt with, and it has the most entertaining meshing process — also the most time-consuming. Despite CFD requiring an equally refined mesh, structural meshing with Hypermesh felt more user-friendly and less likely to send you in circles.

After meshing comes the real engineering: organising the file, numbering nodes, elements, and assemblies, tracking materials, and deciding how to model screws (always critical — failure usually initiates near fasteners).

Types of analysis I worked with:

  • Linear Static: Equilibrium under steady loads, linear material behaviour, small deformations.
  • Modal (Normal Modes): Natural frequencies and mode shapes — essential to avoid resonance.
  • Harmonic / Frequency Response (Sine): Steady-state response to sinusoidal excitation across a frequency range. Useful for understanding the effect of engine vibrations.
  • Transient Time-History: Time-domain dynamic simulation for arbitrary load histories (impulses, step loads).
  • Random Vibration (PSD): Response to a broad spectrum using a Power Spectral Density approach — for chaotic inputs like a rocket launch.
  • Shock / Impulse: Short-duration, high-amplitude loads.
  • Quasi-Static Load (QSL): Slowly applied loads where inertia is negligible.

There are many more — buckling, nonlinear, thermostructural, fatigue, contact, topology optimisation — but those are for the next chapter!

02 · Thermal

Thermal Engineering

Thermal was the last branch I worked with. I had less time to experiment, but it was still very valuable. Working with ESATAN revealed the key differences from structural work: the mesh is much coarser — large elements and 2D shells representing moderate-width objects. The meshing effort is simplified, but defining accurate linear conductors takes its place.

Similar to electrical circuits, thermal paths can be represented by thermal resistances in series or parallel. Accurately modelling the critical paths from heat-dissipating components to the environment lets you determine the limit temperatures the system will reach.

In space, with no convection, radiation is the only heat-rejection mechanism. ESATAN automatically computes Radiative Exchange Factors (REFs) from exposed surfaces and optical properties, and can model orbits, eclipses, Earth's albedo, and other orbital phenomena. On the ground, convective coefficients also need estimating — often requiring CFD, empirical correlations, or good old reference books.

Beyond conductors and radiation, thermal models also include heaters, thermostats, and internal dissipations — all to keep every component within its operational temperature limits.

03 · CFD

Computational Fluid Dynamics

By far my favourite branch. This period at MSE led to my bachelor's thesis: Design and Simulation of a Micronozzle for In-Space Propulsion. I used NASA's CEA, CoolProp, MATLAB, and above all OpenFOAM. Working in CFD varies enormously between software — commercial tools like Ansys are user-friendly but expensive; open-source tools have a steeper learning curve but far greater customisation. If you want to do real CFD, the UI is the last of your concerns.

A solid understanding of fluid dynamics is non-negotiable. More than in any other simulation discipline, you must interpret results, perform sanity checks, and understand what every variable actually means. The real first step is meshing:

  • Geometry simplification: Remove small features that complicate the mesh without affecting the flow.
  • Boundary layer refinement: Target the correct y⁺ (usually ~1, depending on the turbulence model), keep a growth factor below 1.3, and use a consistent number of layers.
  • Mesh sensitivity analysis: Refine progressively until the solution no longer changes — that is mesh independence.
  • Mesh quality checks: Evaluate skewness, aspect ratio, and orthogonality using built-in tools.

A good mesh is only the beginning. You must also choose the solver, turbulence model, governing equations, discretisation scheme, boundary conditions, and initial conditions. For boundary conditions specifically, there is no substitute for understanding the physics.

Two tips I carry from this experience: if you expect supersonic flow, never impose conditions at the outlet — let the solution extrapolate from the interior (Mach characteristic lines). And a well-applied symmetry condition can cut simulation time from a week to a day.

CFD's breadth is what makes it endlessly interesting — airfoils, rockets, buildings, turbomachinery, nozzles — and you can extract virtually any fluid-related quantity. That variety means it never gets boring. The same could be said for the other two disciplines, honestly.

I could talk for hours about CFD and simulation in general. But the main thing I want to convey is the amazing range of possibilities in the simulations domain — especially when disciplines are coupled (thermostructural, conjugate heat transfer…). The possibilities are endless. I am no expert, and everything here is personal and subjective. But I hope it helps you understand what this branch of engineering really looks like — and maybe helps you find your own passion, as I found mine.
